Which pages should a content team review first?

A grouped-validation study of click opportunity
Aleeza Nadeem · Machine Learning track, FlyRank internship · October 2026
Lane: click opportunity / CTR · Status: observed, measured, directional; decision support only

Abstract

This paper asks which pages will earn the most search clicks in the next 30 days, so that a content team knows where a title or description review is worth the effort first. I used 26,612 pages from 31 anonymized clients, with features taken from the previous 30 days and the label taken from the following 30 days. I compared a transparent Week-4 opportunity rule, a “rank by last month’s clicks” persistence check, Logistic Regression and a Random Forest, all scored on whole clients held out from training. The learned models reached a ROC AUC of 0.975 against 0.366 for the opportunity rule, but the persistence check reached 0.968, so the models add little beyond last month’s clicks. The output is a ranked review queue of 5,378 under-clicked pages with reason codes and human-review rules; it is decision support for people, not an automated or causal tool.

1. Introduction and problem statement

A content team has limited review time and thousands of pages. The unit of analysis here is a page. The output is a ranked list of pages to look at first, each with a reason code and a suggested first action. A person decides what to do. A wrong call costs review time, and, if acted on without checking, a rewrite that may not help. Data helps because no one can read every page’s search history, but the output is only useful if its claims are honest, so this paper puts the baseline, the validation design and the limits up front.

2. Data

The data is the anonymized FlyRank ML Internship content-refresh table: 30,000 page rows from 31 clients. I kept 26,612 rows after dropping rows with missing key fields (clicks, impressions, sessions, position, client) or zero impressions in the previous 30 days.

  • Backward window (inputs): previous-30-day impressions, clicks and sessions; search volume, competition, CPC, word count, content age and days since last update; a 90-day average position; content type and main intent.
  • Forward window (label): clicks in the last 30 days of the table. A page is positive if it is in the top 20% for those clicks, with the cut-off (5 clicks) set on training clients only.
  • Left out on purpose: every forward-window column except the label source, optimization flags, the health score and trend fields (they are rule-made or label-derived), and identifiers. Client ID is used only to group the split. Page IDs are never features.
  • Public safety: no client names, page addresses or search queries appear in this paper or in the notebook outputs. Content-type labels are anonymized.

3. Methodology

Baseline and comparison methods

  1. Week-4 opportunity rule: estimated clicks left on the table = previous impressions × (typical CTR for the page’s position bucket − its own CTR), floored at zero. Typical CTR is the median per bucket on training clients.
  2. Persistence check: rank pages by last month’s clicks. Any model must beat this to have learned something.
  3. Logistic Regression: log-scaled, standardized features, class-balanced. A readable linear model.
  4. Random Forest: 300 trees, minimum 20 pages per leaf, class-balanced. Read with permutation importance.

Validation design

The split is grouped by client (75% / 25%, seed 42): 23 clients and 20,586 pages for training, 8 unseen clients and 6,026 pages for testing. A random row split would let a client’s habits leak from training into test. Missing numeric values are filled with training-row medians. The test set’s positive rate is 13.3% (training 20.1%), which is the base rate any precision figure must be read against. Metrics: Precision@100, Precision@500, PR AUC and ROC AUC, identical for every method.

Leakage checks

  • No banned column names (forward-window, flag, health-score or trend fields) in the feature set.
  • Strongest single features on held-out clients: previous clicks (AUC 0.968), previous impressions (0.961), previous sessions (0.930). They come from the earlier window, so I read them as persistence, not leakage.
  • Shuffled-label control: AUC 0.501, close to chance. No page appears in both train and test.
  • Before and after: a random row split shares all 31 clients between train and test (Precision@100 1.00, ROC AUC 0.971); the grouped split shares none (0.95, 0.975). The honest split did not lower AUC, so the models do not seem to depend on client habits here.

Known caveat: the 90-day average position overlaps the label window, a mild window overlap used by the baseline and the models alike.

Both learned models beat the Week-4 rule by a wide margin. But the persistence check lands within 0.007 ROC AUC of them, and its Precision@100 and Precision@500 match or beat the Random Forest. In this sample the models add little beyond persistence, and the Random Forest’s extra complexity did not earn its place. The Week-4 rule scores poorly because it was built to find under-clicked pages, not pages that will have the most clicks, so it answers a different question than this label. Precision@100 is saturated near 1.00, which is why Precision@500 and PR AUC are shown.

What the model leans on, and where it is wrong

Permutation importance (drop in ROC AUC when a feature is shuffled) is 0.094 for previous clicks and 0.034 for previous impressions; sessions score 0.004 and every other feature 0.001 or less. Of the Random Forest’s top 100 picks, 4 were wrong: they had a median of about 22 clicks in the earlier month but only about 2 in the next, so their clicks fell sharply. The true top pages it missed had a median of 9 earlier clicks, close to the 5-click cut-off, which makes them hard to separate. The test clients’ positive rate (13.3%) is lower than training (20.1%), so held-out clients differ somewhat.

“Clicks left” is a ceiling on 30-day clicks, not a forecast, and the review time is an assumption. Returns fall as the list grows, so a team can stop where the hours stop being worth it.

A person checks before acting

  1. Confirm the page’s CTR and position in its own search data (a CTR near zero on very high impressions is the first thing to doubt).
  2. Check whether the searches are branded or navigational, where low CTR is normal.
  3. Look at the results page: a snippet, ad or AI answer may take the clicks.
  4. Read the current title and description and decide whether a rewrite is needed.
  5. Record the change date so later movement is reviewed fairly, not assumed to be caused by the change.

What should not be automated

Publishing or rewriting titles, descriptions or content without approval; deleting, merging or redirecting LOW_VISIBILITY pages; reporting results as proof that a refresh worked; using the queue on a new site without re-checking the data and model; sharing client names, page addresses, queries or raw exports.

Monitoring and retraining

  • Review the model if ROC AUC on newly arrived pages falls below about 0.90 (lowest validated fold 0.945, minus 0.05).
  • Rebuild the typical-CTR table if any position bucket’s median CTR shifts by more than about a quarter.
  • Re-run the leakage audit after a new data release, client mix or columns.
  • Add a rule if reviewers keep rejecting rows for one reason; re-check at least once per quarter.

Decay and refresh, descriptively

The median recent trend by days since last update was −41.4% (0–20 days), −13.5% (20–60), −34.4% (60–120) and −46.0% (120–400, only 162 pages); most pages declined in every group. Staleness is a mixed signal here, and this is association only. It does not show that a refresh causes recovery.

6. Limitations and honest framing

  • Small, anonymized sample: about 30,000 pages from 31 clients; results on other sites or the full warehouse may differ.
  • One grouped split, 8 held-out clients: the numbers could move with a different split. The five-fold client-grouped check gave ROC AUC 0.945 to 0.986.
  • Mostly persistence: last month’s clicks nearly match the models, so the model chance is a mild re-weighting of the queue, not independent evidence.
  • Window overlap: the 90-day average position overlaps the label window.
  • Typical CTR is crude: medians per position bucket are near zero in this data, so “under-clicked” is a relative flag, not a verdict; CTR can be low for reasons a title cannot fix.
  • Medians over all pages: the typical-CTR table and the median fills in the playbook use all pages, fine for a planning queue but not a held-out evaluation.
  • No causal claim: the data shows association only. It does not show that a rewrite or refresh causes more clicks, and it says nothing about how Google ranks pages.

I audited my own claims with the same questions I would ask of any report: where does the label come from, does the validation carry the claim, and is a simple baseline compared on the same split?
